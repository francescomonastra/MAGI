## 1. The short answer

The field expects **seven layers** of validation from a released generative
surrogate. MAGI is strong on layers 1, 2, 5 and 6 — in places above field norm —
and has **two conspicuous holes** (layer 3's classifier test, layer 4's
memorization test) that are both cheap and both directly aimed at questions
MAGI's own results invite. Layer 0 (release mechanics) is **not started**: no
LICENSE, no tag, no DOI.

Before the conference, the binding constraint is your own pre-committed rule —
*no model work before 14/08*, and the abort criterion in
[roadmap §8](v0.8.2_RoadmapForAdoption.md). So **every pre-conference item below
is either release hygiene or a measurement on checkpoints that already exist.**
Nothing on the list requires a training run.

The single largest risk is not a validation gap at all: **the speed-up number
does not exist yet.** GPU *training* wall-clock does exist — see §2.5 — but the
comparison that justifies the tool, MAGI generation against full Geant4 transport
at matched statistics, has not been measured anywhere. §`sec:cost` of
`magi_csbs.tex` is `[FILL]` and nothing on disk or on Drive fills it.

Working against that, §2.5 also records a **large asset that was not in the local
repo**: two further sources, Torio and Radio, trained on Colab GPU and fully
scored. They answer the paper's own "validation on a third source" limitation and
they break the confound that has blocked roadmap hypothesis (b) all month.

## 2. The seven layers, as the field defines them

### Layer 0 — Release mechanics (FAIR4RS / JOSS)

The FAIR Principles for Research Software [B22] and the JOSS review checklist
[JOSS] converge on the same short list: an OSI licence in a plain-text file, a
tagged release deposited with an archiving service for a **DOI**, `CITATION.cff`,
automated tests, installation instructions verified on a clean environment, API
documentation, a statement of need, semantic versioning, a changelog, and
community/contribution guidelines.

This is not optional garnish for a proceedings — the paper currently has four
`[FILL licence]` / `[FILL Zenodo]` slots that cannot be closed any other way.

### Layer 1 — Verification (is the code solving the intended maths?)

The ASME VVUQ framing [ASME] separates *verification* (the code implements the
mathematical model) from *validation* (the model represents reality). The
standard verification instruments are round-trip/invertibility identities,
conservation checks, and **problems with a known analytic answer**.

KDSource [S22] — the closest published analogue to MAGI, a KDE-based phase-space
resampler for Monte Carlo transport — is the template here: it validated on "an
analytical problem with a known density distribution" *before* the two real
neutron-beam cases, and reported agreement to 2% (fluence), 1.5% (energy) and
0.1% (angular).

### Layer 2 — Marginal distributional validation

1-D histograms with residuals, per observable, plus a scalar distance.
CaloChallenge 2022 [K24] used 1-D histogram differences, KL divergence and χ².
Kansal et al. [K23] is the load-bearing methodological reference: they evaluate
metrics *as metrics* — sensitivity to mismodelling, bias, and **sample-size
dependence** — and recommend Wasserstein-1 alongside their FPD/KPD. The practical
consequence they push is that a distance quoted without its own noise floor is
not a result.

### Layer 3 — Joint / high-dimensional validation

This is where the field has moved, and where marginals are known to be
insufficient.

- **Classifier two-sample test (C2ST)** [LP16]: train a classifier to separate
  real from generated; AUC = 0.5 means indistinguishable, AUC → 1 means the
  classifier found structure the marginals didn't show. CaloChallenge adopted
  exactly this ("AUCs of binary classifiers") plus a multiclass classifier
  log-posterior for ranking several models at once [K24].
- **FPD / KPD** [K23]: Fréchet and kernel distances over hand-engineered physics
  features; FPD was the most sensitive metric to every mismodelling they tested.
- **Calibrated goodness-of-fit**: NPLM [C26] turns the classifier idea into an
  actual likelihood-ratio test with a null distribution built from ~200
  pseudo-experiments, so the output is a Z-score rather than a number without a
  scale. Their sample-size guidance for 16-D jet data is ~10k / 40k / 70k test
  samples for 1σ / 3σ / 5σ sensitivity.

### Layer 4 — Generative-model pathologies

Two failure modes that distributional metrics do not separate:

- **Fidelity vs diversity.** Precision/recall variants, and Naeem et al.'s
  density/coverage [N20], split "the samples are plausible" from "the samples
  cover the real support". Low recall with high precision is the standard
  signature of mode collapse.
- **Memorization / data-copying.** Meehan et al. [M20] give a three-sample
  non-parametric test (train / test / generated) whose statistic asks how often
  training points are closer to generated samples than test points are — a
  Mann-Whitney test, not an eyeball nearest-neighbour montage, which the
  memorization literature specifically warns is misleading [vdB21, WH19].

### Layer 5 — Physics and downstream validation

The theme running through the production-deployment literature is that the
surrogate is judged on **the observable the downstream analysis actually uses**,
not on the intermediate distributions. AtlFast3 [A22] — one of the first
generative models in production in a large HEP experiment — selects between
FastCaloSimV2 and FastCaloGAN per shower and is tuned "to optimise the
performance of reconstructed observables, including those exploiting jet
substructure." The taxonomic review [HK24] states the requirement as samples
"faithful and diverse from the downstream physics analysis point of view."

For this instrument domain specifically, the Athena X-IFU background review
[L21] reports that the underlying Geant4 predictions themselves carry a **~20%
systematic** from Geant4's reproducibility of in-flight background data
(attributed there to Ozaki & Fioretti 2018), and that the physics models were
refined "through extensive validations against experimental results."

### Layer 6 — Uncertainty and stability

Multi-seed reporting is now the expected default: train ≥3 models with different
seeds, report mean and σ, and compare any claimed gain against the spread caused
by randomness alone [Mü20, HIB17]. Recent work continues to find single-seed
evaluation unreliable at the tens-of-percent level [A25].

### Layer 7 — Cost

CaloChallenge [K24] compares submissions on **shower generation time and model
size** alongside fidelity. A fast-simulation paper without a timing number
against the code it replaces, with training amortized, is not making its claim.

`MyDrive/MAGI_data/trained_models/` holds three v0.8.2 Colab-GPU checkpoints —

Both clear the distribution-level bar. For scale, the CPU reference is ~90 min for

### One honest flag

Radio's **raw φ_v marginal is W1 = 0.0549**, over the 0.05 bar (its quantile
counterpart φ_v_q is 0.0407, and cphi_v 0.0407). The bar as written in
`tab:accept` applies to log₁₀E, which Radio passes at 0.0104 — but if Radio goes
in the paper, that φ_v number should go in with it.

**Every line above ~4e-4 is in band** (the two exceptions are CR's 511 and CR's
Cu Kα₁, already diagnosed as separate mechanisms in
[roadmap §5.2/§6](v0.8.2_RoadmapForAdoption.md)). **Every line below ~1e-4 is
over-generated by 2.5–4.5×**, on all four sources, with no exception.

The reason this matters now and did not before: [roadmap §5.2(b)](v0.8.2_RoadmapForAdoption.md)
flagged the confound that "rarest" and "Small" were almost the same set, so a
fraction effect could not be told from a source/dataset-size effect. **Torio breaks
it.** Torio has 8.85 M crossings — 2.6× CR, 4.7× Small — and its three sub-1e-4
lines still over-generate 2.6–4.0× while its 1.5e-2 line lands at 1.038. Dataset
size is not the driver; component fraction is.

That converts the paper's largest weakness into a stated scope boundary:
**MAGI reproduces line intensities for components down to a fraction of roughly
10⁻⁴, and systematically over-routes below it.** The roadmap already anticipated
this as "a methodological result worth publishing, not merely a limitation" — the
evidence for it now exists and is sitting on Drive.

## 5. Three framing arguments the literature hands you for free

**(a) The pre-registered bar is above field norm.** CaloChallenge, the most
comprehensive survey in this space, reports metrics; it does not fix two-sided
pass/fail thresholds in advance. MAGI has six criteria fixed before the runs, a
two-sided line band, and a 5σ Poisson detection floor borrowed from the discovery
convention. Lead the validation section with that, because it reframes the line
failure from "the tool doesn't work" to "the tool has a bar and reports where it
sits against it."

**(b) ~20% is the domain's floor, not MAGI's failure.** The X-IFU background
review [L21] puts a ~20% systematic on the Geant4 predictions themselves, from
Geant4's reproducibility of in-flight background data. MAGI's ~20–24% CR
detector-level residual is *at the level of the irreducible simulation
systematic in this domain*. Claim "at the level of", never "better than" — but
do claim it, because without that context 20% reads as a large error rather than
as the noise floor of the enterprise.

**(c) You have a downstream closure test, which most ML fast-sim papers don't.**
Feeding a MAGI-generated source back into Geant4 and comparing the detector-level
observable (K-40: 1.118 ± 0.373, KS p = 0.60) is the AtlFast3 philosophy [A22] —
judge the surrogate on the reconstructed observable — applied at a scale one
person can run. The taxonomic review [HK24] names this as the requirement and the
benchmark literature mostly doesn't meet it. This is a stronger talk point than
any single distance metric.

**(d) A scope boundary beats a failure list.** §2.5 turns "5 of 7 lines are out of
band" into "line intensities are reproduced down to a component fraction of ~10⁻⁴,
below which the gate over-routes by 2.5–4.5×, measured on four sources spanning
1.4 M to 8.8 M crossings." Same data, and the second version tells a user of the
tool what they can rely on. It is also the form the hypothesis-(b) discussion in
[roadmap §5.2](v0.8.2_RoadmapForAdoption.md) predicted would be publishable.

**One caution to pair with them.** The honest reporting of line-intensity failure
and of three negative results is an asset only if the *coverage* of testing looks
complete. "We report what failed" invites "what didn't you test?" — which is
precisely why items 9 and 10 are worth a day. They are the two tests an informed
listener will know are missing.
